## Post 1. The council that shows zero

Cherbourg has 1,264 people and sits in the most disadvantaged tenth of Australia. Our register can
place one organisation there. Another 114 share its postcode, and nothing in any public record says
which side of the line they are on, so we leave them unplaced and print the number.

That is the honest version of a funding map. Most maps you will see this week do the other thing.
They put a dot on the hub town where the intermediary keeps its address and call the community
funded.

We have just put every council in Australia on one line: how disadvantaged it is, how many people
live there, what the charities placed there reported receiving from government and from donors in
their latest ACNC statement, and how sure we are of the row. 546 councils. $82 billion from
government and $10.8 billion in donations and bequests, from the charities we could put on a
council line. The register as a whole reported $107 billion and $18.5 billion for 2023; the gap is
organisations with no council we can stand behind, and it is printed, not hidden.

The eight councils at the bottom of the need scale with the least visible money are all in
Queensland, all Aboriginal shires: Torres Strait Island, Aurukun, Palm Island, Doomadgee, Hope
Vale, Kowanyama, Cherbourg, Yarrabah. Their rows read between 1% and 25% sure. Read that as a
statement about the record, not about the place. Money follows the recipient's address, and the
address is usually somewhere else.

We tried the other lane. GrantConnect lets an agency say where a grant will be delivered, and some
do. The Australian Research Council gives a delivery postcode on every dollar. In the last two years
the National Indigenous Australians Agency published $3.84 billion of grants, with a delivery state
on every one and a delivery postcode on none: 79 say "Multiple", the rest are blank. Health,
Disability and Ageing gave a postcode on 2% of its $21.4 billion. The agencies that fund the places
at the bottom of this list are the ones that do not say where the money lands. So the gap between
the hub and the community stays a story told by address, and we print the coverage beside the
figure so you can see how much of it is the record staying quiet.

If you fund in those places, tell us which organisation is yours. The council page has a form.
We will correct the row and say who corrected it.

## Post 2. You fund a snapshot. The organisation is a line.

Every charity files a statement a year. Read one and you get a size. Read seven and you get a
direction.

We read 2017 to 2023 for every ABN on the ACNC register, 63,565 organisations, and sorted the
register by what moved.

12,114 have lost more than a fifth of their revenue between first and latest statement. 3,919 ran
a deficit in each of their last three. 7,431 take seventy cents or more of every dollar from
government, which means one contract ending is the organisation ending. 6,057 have filed nothing
since 2021, and we do not know which of those closed.

None of that is in a grant application, because an application is written about the year ahead.
It is all in the returns, in public, and nobody was reading it as a line.

For a funder this is portfolio risk with the names attached. For a community organisation it is
the argument for the multi-year grant you have been making in the "other comments" box for a
decade. For us it is the second surface in a week that reads every row on the register.

Each charity's page now carries its own trajectory: direction, government share then and now,
donation share then and now, deficits in the last three, months of reserves. Look yours up before
someone else does.

## Post 3. What philanthropy actually did for Goods

We run a line called Confessions to Philanthropy. The tag is "not anti-philanthropy, anti
pretending". So here is the un-pretended version of our own money.

Goods makes beds and washing machines with remote communities so the making, and the margin, stay
there. The grant and philanthropic receipts on the books this month total $556,330, from The Snow
Foundation, the Vincent Fairfax Family Foundation, QIC and The John Villiers Trust. Nothing is
signed for the next round. We say that every time, because the alternative is a deck with a pipeline slide.

The single most useful thing a foundation did for Goods this year was pay an invoice. Centrecorp
Foundation bought 130 beds, two orders, $123,332, delivered to Utopia. On 5 September we moved that
money out of the philanthropy column and into the buyer column, because the paper is an invoice and
one dollar gets one label. A foundation that buys the product is doing something a grant cannot: it
is proving the price.

Snow moved first. First money is worth more than the amount, because everyone after asks who else
is in. The QBE Catalysing Impact ask on the table is
$400,000, and every dollar of it buys beds at $750, which is 533 beds, the first two communities'
pools and a start on the third.

That is the whole relationship. Four funders, one buyer, one open ask, nothing signed, and a
register we built so that anyone can check us the way we check everyone else.

Come and confess. The line is open.
